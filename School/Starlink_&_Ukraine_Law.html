Starlink in plain words

Starlink is internet from space. Since February 2022 it has been one of the most important tools Ukraine has. Understanding how it works explains how one switch can change a war.

  1. The dish finds a satellite. A flat dish about the size of a pizza box (the Starlink Mini is smaller) steers a narrow radio beam at a satellite passing overhead. The narrow beam makes it hard to jam.
  2. Satellites pass the signal along. More than 10,000 satellites fly in low orbit. Some pass data to each other by laser, so the signal can cross a war zone in the sky.
  3. A ground station connects it. A satellite beams the data down to a gateway, sometimes in another country, which plugs into ordinary internet cables.
  4. The dish reports where it is. Each dish knows its location, identity and speed. That is what lets SpaceX switch service on or off, very precisely.

The three switches SpaceX holds

Because every dish reports its place, its ID and its speed, the company running the network can make three kinds of decisions from its control room. In 2026 all three were used: first against Russia, then against Ukraine.

Where: geofencing

Service can be turned off in whole areas. Starlink has never been active inside Russia, so a dish carried there simply does not work.

Used for: keeping Starlink dark inside Russia since 2022.

Who: the whitelist

Only dishes on an approved list get service. Since February 2026, a dish in Ukraine only works if Ukraine's Defence Ministry has registered it.

Used for: cutting off Russian troops using smuggled dishes (February 2026).

How fast: the speed cap

A dish moving faster than a set speed loses its link. That stops it working on fast drones, while dishes on the ground keep working.

Used for: grounding Russian strike drones (January 2026), then slowing Ukraine's own interceptors (October 2026).

How Russia ended up using Starlink

SpaceX has never sold Starlink to Russia. Russian forces got it anyway, through smuggling, and used it until the whitelist shut them out.

1. Bought abroad

Dishes are bought in third countries. In 2024 Ukrainian intelligence aired an intercept of Russian soldiers discussing buying them in Arab countries for about 200,000 rubles each.

2. Smuggled in

“Parallel import” networks move them to the front, past US export controls.

3. Switched on over Ukraine

Starlink is off inside Russia, but it covers Ukraine. Dishes used in occupied areas or near the front could connect.

4. Strapped to drones

Starlink Minis were mounted on Shahed, Molniya and BM-35 strike drones. A satellite link resists jamming and lets a pilot steer from far away.

5. Cut off

After a January 2026 drone strike on a passenger train, SpaceX and Ukraine built the whitelist. A Russian official admitted Russian systems were down for two weeks.

Is it illegal for Russia to use Starlink? Not by itself, under international law. Using a smuggled radio is not a war crime. It breaks US export rules and SpaceX's terms. Under the law of war, what matters is what the drones hit. Steering a drone into a civilian passenger train is the likely crime, whatever the drone uses to steer.

    The law, word for word

    International law is mostly a set of rules between countries. Companies and people are covered mainly by their own country's laws and by war-crimes law. Keep that in mind: it decides the Musk question.

    Is Musk breaking international law?

    Follow the legal test step by step. The answer is the same whichever report about who gave the order turns out to be true.

    October 13–14, 2026

    Starshield stops working for Ukrainian terminals moving faster than about 161 km/h. Interceptor drones fail during Russian attacks.

    Who gave the order?
    Reuters, Oct 15: the White House

    Then it is US government conduct

    Acts of officials count as acts of the state. If SpaceX only carried out their instruction, that also counts as US conduct.

    WSJ and CBS, Oct 21: Musk

    Then it is private conduct, but the US still answers

    A private choice is not the state's act. But the US answers internationally for its companies' space activities and must supervise them.

    Did it break a rule binding the US?

    No

    No treaty or custom obliges the US, or SpaceX, to keep supplying Starshield. The 2024 US–Ukraine pact says its annex creates no legal obligations. Neutrality law says outsiders do not have to provide radio links at all.

    Did anyone help Russia commit a crime, on purpose?

    No evidence of that

    Slowing the victim's link is not, in law, helping the attacker. Complicity needs help that contributes to a specific wrong, and knowledge or purpose. War-crimes liability needs the purpose of facilitating a crime.

    Result: no breach of international law

    But one company boss or one office can quietly weaken an ally's air defence in the middle of an attack, without notice, and no international rule stops it. That is a gap in the law, not a breach of it.

    What would change this answer

    • Evidence the cap was timed to help a specific Russian strike, with that purpose. That could engage complicity rules and, for individuals, war-crimes law.
    • A published contract or treaty that guarantees Ukraine uninterrupted service. Breaking it would then be a legal wrong, under contract law or the treaty.
    • A US decision to treat the war as neutral ground. Then Hague V's equal-treatment rule (Art. 9) would apply, and restricting only Ukraine would be a problem.

    Where could this actually be decided?

    Breaking a rule and getting a ruling are different things. Most international courts need the accused country's consent.

    My stance

    Russia is the law-breaker. The United States is using lawful pressure in ways that weaken the victim. Musk has not broken international law, and that is exactly what the law now needs to fix.

    Clear breach

    On Russia

    The invasion breaks the most basic rule in the UN Charter. Russia defies a binding World Court order. ICC judges see reasonable grounds for war crimes in its strikes on Ukraine's power grid. Its use of smuggled Starlinks is not the crime; what those drones hit is.

    Lawful, but corrosive

    On the United States

    No rule forces a country to keep helping another. The diesel deal and the cap do not meet the legal test for complicity. The real legal limits are ahead: no recognition of land taken by force, and no deal that binds Ukraine without its consent. The live questions today are under US law.

    No breach, a gap

    On Musk and SpaceX

    Most of these rules bind states, not companies. The Outer Space Treaty puts the duty on Washington to supervise SpaceX. A 1907 neutrality treaty shows the law once cared about private wartime networks, but it was written for telegraphs, and it does not fit.

    The strongest arguments against my view

    “Ensure respect” creates a duty to keep helping

    The Red Cross reads Common Article 1 as requiring outside countries to do what they reasonably can to stop violations. Deliberately degrading a uniquely effective air-defence tool during attacks on civilians could fail that test.

    My response

    This is the best argument. But several states reject the broad reading, and no court has applied it to cutting support for a victim. It is a serious legal argument, not yet settled law.

    The diesel deal funds the war, so the US is aiding Russia

    Lifting sanctions puts money in Russia's budget, which pays for missiles that hit cities.

    My response

    Article 16 needs help that contributes to a specific wrongful act. General revenue is too far removed, and there is no duty to impose sanctions in the first place.

    Ukraine relied on Starshield in good faith

    The US encouraged Ukraine to build its defence around Starshield. Cutting it without warning breaks the spirit of good faith.

    My response

    Good faith applies to binding promises (VCLT Art. 26). The public agreements contain none for Starshield. This is a strong political and moral point, but a weak legal one.

    Cutting off only Russia broke neutrality first

    Hague V says a neutral that restricts radio links must restrict both sides equally.

    My response

    The US is not neutral toward a state that 141 countries named as an aggressor. Many lawyers accept this “qualified neutrality.” And Hague V's own Article 20 limits its reach.

    What should happen next

    1. Find out who gave the order. The Pentagon's inspector general inquiry, requested by senators from both parties, should say who decided and on what authority.
    2. Write notice terms into the contracts. Allies who depend on a private network need a binding promise of warning before any cut, and a named official who must approve it.
    3. Use the Outer Space Treaty as written. Article VI already says the US must authorize and supervise SpaceX. Clear rules for wartime service decisions would make that duty real.
    4. Ask the World Court. The General Assembly can request an advisory opinion (Charter Art. 96) on what states owe when private satellites carry a war.
    5. Build backups. Europe's Govsatcom adds commercial capacity in early 2027 and IRIS² is due around 2029. Until then, Ukraine has no full alternative.

    This is an analysis of public treaty texts and reporting, not legal advice. Who ordered the October cap is still disputed: Reuters points to the White House, the Telegraph to Musk with Trump's agreement, and the Wall Street Journal and CBS to Musk. The contracts behind Starshield are not public.
