The Starshield question

In October 2026, Ukraine's military satellite link was slowed in the middle of Russian attacks. Who did it, and did anyone break the law? A lawyer's walk-through in plain words.

How we got here

Starlink became Ukraine's battlefield internet after Russia invaded in 2022. The Pentagon then started paying for Starshield, the military version. In October 2026, amid a US–Ukraine fight over Ukraine's strikes on Russian oil refineries, that link was slowed. Click through each step.

What was cut: the speed cap

Nothing was switched off. Instead, any Starshield dish moving faster than about 161 km/h (100 mph) lost its link. That sounds technical, but it decides which drones can fly. Drag the speed and switch between periods.

Why a lawyer cares

How a speed cap works

1. The dish moves

A small Starshield dish rides on a drone or vehicle. It keeps a narrow beam locked on a satellite overhead.

2. The network sees its speed

Each dish knows where it is. The network can tell how fast it is moving and who it belongs to.

3. A rule is checked

A setting says: above this speed, stop serving. It is a policy choice, not a hardware limit.

4. The link drops

Too fast, and video and control stop. A drone in the air becomes blind or falls.

SpaceX has not published how the cap was applied. This is the general principle. The same tool, at about 75 km/h, was used in February 2026 to ground Russian drones carrying smuggled Starlinks.

Who it hit, and who it didn't

Air defence

Interceptor drones that chase Russian jet-powered drones. Zelenskyy: the limits “prevent us from defending ourselves and protecting our people.”

This is the strongest fact for the Geneva “ensure respect” argument.
Mid-range strikes

Strike drones that cruise at 160 km/h or more, against Russian forces in occupied Ukraine. One analyst put losses at tens of millions of dollars.

Harm is real, but harm alone is not a legal breach (reading, p. 16).
Front-line supply

Zelenskyy said the cap was “slowing down our frontline logistics.”

Shows the cap was military, not civilian, in effect.
Not refinery strikes

Starlink does not work inside Russia. Drones that hit Russian refineries use other links.

If the aim was to stop refinery strikes, the cap missed. That matters for motive and intent (reading, p. 5).

Who supervises SpaceX for the US?

The Outer Space Treaty says a country must “authorize” and “continually supervise” its companies in space. In the US, that job is split three ways (reading, pp. 8–9):

FCC

Licenses Starlink's satellites and radio spectrum.

FAA

Licenses launches and re-entries only. No say over service after orbit.

Pentagon and Space Force

Controls Starshield use through contracts. This is the real lever, and the gap: on Oct 21 Rubio said, “I'm not aware that we knew about this decision beforehand.”

Background worth knowing: in May 2026 Musk accused the Pentagon of “improper use of the Starlink civilian system for military purposes” on US drones in Iran, calling it a “direct violation of terms of service.” The Pentagon said it was looking for more vendors. Terms-of-service fights between SpaceX and the Pentagon were already live.

What the senators asked the Pentagon's watchdog

Letter of October 15, 2026, from nine senators of both parties (led by Shaheen and Tillis) to the Defense Department Inspector General. They asked for records to be preserved and a briefing by November 1.

  1. Did disruptions to warnings leave US personnel unable to reach shelter during attacks?
  2. Did US officials direct, approve, or know in advance about the changes?
  3. Were the intelligence cut and the Starlink limits connected? Policy, technical failure, or contractor action?
  4. Did US personnel, the embassy and Ukraine get adequate advance notice?
  5. Did SpaceX impose the limits on its own, and did that comply with US government contracts?
  6. What corrective steps are being taken now?

Why this letter is the most important legal document so far

Every legal argument on this page turns on facts nobody has proven yet. Question 2 decides whether it was a US act. Question 5 decides the contract claim. Question 4 decides the notice issue.

International law has no investigator here. The Pentagon's inspector general does. In practice, this inquiry will settle more than any court.

If a US company does it, is it America's act?

Usually, no. A company is not the government. But international law has six routes that can link a company's act to its country, and space is special. Pick a version of events and watch which routes light up.

What law comes first?

There are two different questions here: how international law ranks its rules on paper, and which law actually does the work in a case like this. The ICC is not at the top of either list for Starshield.

1. Peremptory rules (jus cogens)

Nothing can override them, not even a treaty. Example: the ban on aggression, which binds Russia. Starshield does not touch this level.

Vienna Convention, Art. 53 (course file)A treaty is void if, at the time of its conclusion, it conflicts with a peremptory norm of general international law.

2. The UN Charter beats other treaties

UN Charter, Art. 103 (course file)In the event of a conflict between the obligations of the Members of the United Nations under the present Charter and their obligations under any other international agreement, their obligations under the present Charter shall prevail.

3. Specific beats general (lex specialis)

When a special rule covers the situation, it applies first. For Starshield: the Outer Space Treaty's Article VI (special, for space) works alongside the general rules on state responsibility, without cancelling them (reading, p. 11).

ILC Articles on State Responsibility, Art. 55These articles do not apply where and to the extent that the conditions for the existence of an internationally wrongful act or the content or implementation of the international responsibility of a State are governed by special rules of international law.

4. Otherwise: treaties, custom, principles. Cases and scholars only help.

No fixed ranking between an ordinary treaty and custom. Court cases and expert writing, like your reading, are “subsidiary means”: they help find the law but do not make it. And a ruling binds only the parties to that case (Art. 59).

ICJ Statute, Art. 38(1) (course file)The Court, whose function is to decide in accordance with international law such disputes as are submitted to it, shall apply: a. international conventions […]; b. international custom, as evidence of a general practice accepted as law; c. the general principles of law recognized by civilized nations; d. subject to the provisions of Article 59, judicial decisions and the teachings of the most highly qualified publicists of the various nations, as subsidiary means for the determination of rules of law.

No. For Starshield, the ICC comes last.

The International Criminal Court tries people, not countries, for four crimes: genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and aggression. Slowing a satellite link is none of these.

To make someone liable for helping a crime, the Rome Statute needs a real crime and a clear purpose:

Rome Statute, Art. 25(3)(c)For the purpose of facilitating the commission of such a crime, aids, abets or otherwise assists in its commission or its attempted commission, including providing the means for its commission;

The ICC matters a lot in this war: it has warrants out for Russian commanders over strikes on Ukraine's power grid. But for the speed cap, the useful law is state responsibility, the contract and Congress.

Arguments, counterarguments, and replies

Six arguments that the cap was unlawful. For each: the argument, the best defence, then the reply to the defence. Step through each round, then see the verdict.

Two arguments that don't work

“The US helped Russia's attacks” (ILC Art. 16, complicity)

The rule covers helping the wrongdoer commit its wrongful act, knowingly. Slowing the victim's link is withholding help, not giving it. The World Court set a high bar in Bosnia v. Serbia (2007).

“Musk is a war criminal” (Rome Statute)

Needs a war crime and the purpose of facilitating it. Slowing a link is not an attack under the laws of war (reading, p. 14), and there is no evidence of such a purpose.

Two cases to focus on: one story, two legal systems

Iran-Contra is your best domestic case. Its international twin is Nicaragua v. United States (1986): the same events, judged by the World Court. Together they show both tracks of the Starshield question, and which one actually bit.

US domestic law · 1985–1992

Iran-Contra

What happened

Congress banned US intelligence agencies and the Pentagon from funding Nicaragua's Contra rebels. White House national security staff (Oliver North) kept them supplied anyway, through a private network the investigators called “the Enterprise,” partly funded by secret arms sales to Iran.

The law it broke

Boland Amendment, Oct 1984 (as quoted in the Walsh report)During fiscal year 1985, no funds available to the Central Intelligence Agency, the Department of Defense, or any other agency or entity of the United States involved in intelligence activities may be obligated or expended for the purpose or which would have the effect of supporting, directly or indirectly, military or paramilitary operations in Nicaragua by any nation, group, organization, movement, or individual.

What happened next

Hearings 198714 charged11 convicted2 overturned6 pardoned, 1992

Congressional majority report, Nov 18, 1987If the President did not know what his National Security Advisers were doing, he should have.

Lessons for Starshield

  • Private hands, public power: using a private actor blurs who decided. Same problem today.
  • “Who knew?” is the whole case: that is question 2 for the Pentagon's inspector general.
  • “Should have known” mirrors the Outer Space Treaty's duty of continuing supervision.
  • Domestic tools bite (hearings, prosecutors, notice rules), though pardons blunted them.

Two courts of law.
International law · ICJ, June 27, 1986

Nicaragua v. United States

What the Court held

The US broke international law: unlawful force, intervention, mining Nicaragua's harbours. But the US was not responsible for the Contras' own abuses, because it did not have “effective control” of their operations.

Para. 115 (attribution)For this conduct to give rise to legal responsibility of the United States, it would in principle have to be proved that that State had effective control of the military or paramilitary operations in the course of which the alleged violations were committed.

Para. 220 (Geneva Common Article 1)The United States is thus under an obligation not to encourage persons or groups engaged in the conflict in Nicaragua to act in violation of the provisions of Article 3 common to the four 1949 Geneva Conventions.

What happened next

Won by NicaraguaUS withdrew from compulsory jurisdictionNever paid; claim dropped 1991

Lessons for Starshield

  • Paying is not controlling: the Pentagon funding Starshield doesn't make every SpaceX choice a US act. The test is control of the specific act.
  • Common Article 1 binds the US: the Court said so. It is the best hook for “illegal.”
  • Winning isn't enforcing: and today Ukraine couldn't even get the US into court (ICJ Statute Art. 36).

A third, home-grown precedent: Crimea, September 2022. Musk refused to switch on Starlink near Crimea for a Ukrainian naval drone attack. Your reading stresses the difference (p. 17): refusing to extend coverage is not the same as cutting a working service mid-mission. The October 2026 cap is the second kind, which is why it raises harder questions.

Bottom line

The United States answers internationally for SpaceX's space activities, but no clear rule required it to keep Starshield running. So the cap is probably not a breach of international law. The real legal action is at home: the Pentagon contract and Congress's oversight.

Whose act?

Open, but the US is on the hook either way

Reuters' version makes it a US act. The WSJ version makes it a private act. Under Article VI of the Outer Space Treaty, the US answers and must supervise in both.

Which rule was broken?

Probably none, internationally

No treaty promises Ukraine the service. The best argument, Geneva Common Article 1, is serious but untested.

What works?

Contract and Congress

The inspector general's facts, the Space Force contract terms, and the 48-hour reporting rule. The ICC is not the place.

Say it in 30 seconds

“Under the Outer Space Treaty, America answers for SpaceX in space, so ‘Musk did it’ doesn't get Washington off the hook. But to call it illegal you need a rule that was broken, and no treaty promised Ukraine the service. The best argument is the Geneva duty to ‘ensure respect,’ which the World Court applied to the US in Nicaragua. In practice, this gets decided like Iran-Contra: by Congress, the Pentagon's inspector general and the contract, not by an international court.”
