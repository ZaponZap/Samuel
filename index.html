TULIP

TULIP is the five-point summary of Calvinism. The acronym itself showed up pretty late (early 1900s), but the doctrine goes back to the Synod of Dort in 1618–19, when the Reformed churches answered the five objections of the Remonstrants (the followers of Jacobus Arminius).

All five points boil to one thing: salvation is God's work from start to the very end. He diagnoses the disease, he chooses, he pays, he calls, and he keeps. In this truly blessed participation of God's grace, my only role is to be on my knees crying out in gratitude, for I am nothing without God.

T

Total Depravity

also called radical corruption, or total inability

Some people hear "total" and think this is a claim about everyone being as evil as they could possibly be. Not necessarily. "Sin" means "missing the mark," and total depravity describes the depth of our tendency to sin (miss the mark). For every being, it has consumed all our parts—mind, will, desires. So left to ourselves, nobody truly wants God... we'll always miss the mark in saving ourselves... to become holy and united in Christ.

Why I believe it

  1. Scripture calls us dead. As for you, you were dead in your transgressions and sins Eph 2:1. Have you ever seen a dead man make the first move?
  2. The problem goes all the way down to the will. There is no one righteous, not even one; there is no one who understands; there is no one who seeks God Rom 3:10–11. The mind governed by the flesh does not submit to God's law, nor can it do so Rom 8:7. Nor can it do so... read that again.
  3. Jesus says it plainly: No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws them John 6:44. "Can." He's talking about ability there.
  4. I see this as great news. If I cannot save myself, my salvation had been resting on God the entire time, which means I can live my life in peace with this certainty. The Bible asks us to confirm our salvation, which will be displayed in how our lives are lived. Self-examination is important, but is it right to live with neverending anxiety/dread about our fates? It seems better to me to surrender your whole life to God and live with full assurance. One who truly loves the Lord, the Lord will surely save. Thank you, Lord Jesus Christ.
But if we can't choose God, how is it fair to hold us responsible?

This is a caricature of Calvinism. We do still have "free will" in our human sense. Can God create a rock so heavy he cannot lift it? Perhaps so, because that rock appears to be us... humanity. We do exactly what we want to do. Light has come into the world, but people loved darkness instead of light John 3:19. Jonathan Edwards (18th century) called this a moral inability. Picture someone who could walk out of a room any time he wants, but he loves the room too much to ever want to leave. This is what we are. Therefore, the responsibility lies with us. But for the one whom God has saved, the Holy Spirit will regenerate the heart to be willing to accept his grace. In other words, those who are saved are those who have surrendered fully to our God. We come to Christ by the Holy Spirit.

U

Unconditional Election

also called sovereign election

Before the creation of the world, God chose to save particular people. He chose them on the basis of his own grace and nothing else—no foreseen merit, no foreseen faith. Purely grace.

Why I believe it

  1. For he chose us in him before the creation of the world… In love he predestined us for adoption to sonship Eph 1:4–5. Look at the motive Paul gives: in love. Election is God setting his love on people before they ever existed.
  2. Paul says Jacob was chosen before the twins were born or had done anything good or bad… not by works but by him who calls Rom 9:11. And then: It does not, therefore, depend on human desire or effort, but on God's mercy Rom 9:16.
  3. All who were appointed for eternal life believed Acts 13:48. Appointed first... then they believed.
  4. If God picked me because he saw ahead of time that I'd believe, then the deciding factor would be me, and I'd have something to boast about. But Paul says faith itself is the gift of God—not by works, so that no one can boast Eph 2:8–9. So where would my boast even come from?
But Romans 8:29 says "those God foreknew." Doesn't that mean he saw our faith ahead of time?

In Scripture, to "know" someone often means to set your love on them. In Amos 3:2 the Hebrew literally says "known" (the KJV keeps it: You only have I known of all the families of the earth), and the NIV translates it You only have I chosen. When Jesus tells some people "I never knew you" (Matt 7:23), he obviously knew they existed. So when Romans 8:29 says God foreknew, it's talking about people he set his love on ahead of time.

Isn't that unfair?

Paul saw this exact objection coming: What then shall we say? Is God unjust? Not at all! Rom 9:14. Nobody is owed grace. If God gave every one of us what's fair, we'd all be condemned. The real wonder is that anyone gets saved at all.

L

Limited Atonement

better called definite atonement, or particular redemption (honestly, "limited" is the worst name of the five)

When Jesus died, he actually paid for—and secured—the salvation of his people. He went to the cross with names on his heart, and the cross accomplished every single thing it set out to do.

Why I believe it

  1. Jesus tells us who he lays his life down for: I lay down my life for the sheep John 10:15. And to some he says, you do not believe because you are not my sheep John 10:26.
  2. He will save his people from their sins Matt 1:21. Will save. That's a promise with the outcome already settled. Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her Eph 5:25. With your blood you purchased for God persons from every tribe and language and people and nation Rev 5:9.
  3. The double-payment problem (John Owen made this one famous): if Christ paid for every sin of every person—unbelief included—then what's left to punish anyone for? Would God collect the same debt twice?
  4. His work as our priest is one seamless piece. He died for and prays for the same people: I am not praying for the world, but for those you have given me John 17:9.
But what about John 3:16? Or "the sins of the whole world" in 1 John 2:2?

In John's writing, "world" very often means people from every nation—Gentiles as well as Jews. John says Jesus would die not only for that nation but also for the scattered children of God, to bring them together and make them one John 11:52. And Dort itself said Christ's death is of infinite worth and value, abundantly sufficient to expiate the sins of the whole world. Sufficient for all, effective for the elect. That's exactly why the gospel still goes out freely—to absolutely everyone.

I

Irresistible Grace

also called effectual calling

When God calls one of his own, the Holy Spirit goes all the way in. He gives them a new heart, and they come running—willingly, gladly.

Why I believe it

  1. God does the heart surgery himself: I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit in you; I will remove from you your heart of stone and give you a heart of flesh Ezek 36:26.
  2. All those the Father gives me will come to me John 6:37. Will come. Jesus talks about it as a certainty.
  3. Lydia: The Lord opened her heart to respond to Paul's message Acts 16:14. First the Lord opened... then she responded.
  4. New birth is something that happens to you. We're born not of natural descent, nor of human decision or a husband's will, but born of God John 1:13. Who has ever caused their own birth?
  5. The Westminster Confession puts it beautifully. We come most freely, being made willing by his grace WCF 10.1.
But Stephen says "You always resist the Holy Spirit!" (Acts 7:51)

Absolutely. People resist the outward call of the gospel all the time. I did—for years. "Irresistible" is about the inward call, the one where the Spirit actually changes the heart. When God sets out to give someone new life, it happens.

P

Perseverance of the Saints

or as I like to think of it, the preservation of the saints

Everyone God truly saves, he keeps. True believers won't finally fall away, because God himself is holding on to them... and he doesn't let go.

Why I believe it

  1. I give them eternal life, and they shall never perish; no one will snatch them out of my hand John 10:28.
  2. The "golden chain": those he predestined, he also called; those he called, he also justified; those he justified, he also glorified Rom 8:30. Nobody drops out between the links. And notice—Paul puts "glorified" in the past tense. That's how certain it is.
  3. He who began a good work in you will carry it on to completion Phil 1:6.
  4. If God did the saving from start to finish (T, U, L, I), it'd be strange for the keeping to suddenly fall on me.
But people do fall away. And what about the warnings in Hebrews 6?

John answers the first part: They went out from us, but they did not really belong to us. For if they had belonged to us, they would have remained with us 1 John 2:19. And the warnings in Hebrews are real. God uses them as one of the very means of keeping his people going. So this doctrine gives zero licence for "once saved, always saved, so live however you want." Real saints persevere, in faith and in holiness, because the Saviour keeps working in them.

Why this matters to me

My own trauma, myopic worldviews, my uncertainties, anxieties, my selfish desires, and lack of care for my well-being... ultimately all boiled down to my lack of true faith in God. All of these reasons are not necessarily excuses, but rather they are the output of a lack of faith rather than a cause of it. And everything is holistic. And with the trauma that is left, what do I make of it? For me, I find peace in knowing that God had been holding on to me all along. It was never me or my choice. God saved me from death. Salvation is His work from start to the very end. Glory to God.

Everything to God

Mental health. Faith. And Romans 12.

Why I care about this

There's a topic I feel quite passionate about, which is the connection between mental health and faith. I think there are going to be people that could benefit from what I want to say.

It's quite personal for me, because for much of my life I had many mental health struggles w/ depression and other coinciding things. It's really been these last 2–3ish years where I've felt like I could operate with the rest of society, coinciding with my return to faith after having abandoned it. My depression faded away as my faith returned.

The main idea

Taking care of your mental well-being—which also includes your physical well-being—is a part of being able to serve God (i.e. worshipping God). Look at what Paul actually tells us to offer: our bodies. And he calls that worship. Your sleep, your meals, your mind... all of it goes on the altar.

Worship does not end at Church. Everything that consists of your life, every action that you take and do, that is all an act of worship.

Sometimes, people are going to perceive their struggles as "distance from God," or they'll hate themselves for having mental health problems. Depression, for example, makes it harder to work to the best of your ability... and then they may think it's because of their distance from God that it's this way.

Or even more simply, someone may not be getting good sleep, or eating enough, and they'll feel more negatively about everything—which doesn't necessarily mean worship and pray more (that definitely helps of course!) but to simply live in a more healthy manner.

Why, my soul, are you downcast? Why so disturbed within me? Put your hope in God, for I will yet praise him, my Savior and my God.

Psalm 42:11

The psalmist wrote that while he was talking to God, right in the middle of it. Even a downcast soul can still talk to him.

My favourite example: Elijah

Right after his biggest victory on Mount Carmel, Elijah runs for his life, sits down under a bush, and asks God to let him die: I have had enough, LORD… Take my life 1 Kings 19:4. So what does God do? Watch this.

1. SleepThen he lay down under the bush and fell asleep. God lets him sleep first.
2. FoodAn angel wakes him up: Get up and eat, for the journey is too much for you (19:7). He eats... and sleeps again.
3. Then the wordAfter all that, God meets him on the mountain in a gentle whisper (19:12).

Sleep. Food. Then God speaks. That order tells me a lot about how God made us... and how patient he is with us.

Holy Communion

My notes on the Lord's Supper. Christ is spiritually present when a Christian takes the wine and bread. But the bread is not literally Christ's body. This is my line of reasoning.

The four views

Protestants rejected transubstantiation during the Reformation... but they disagreed among themselves. Tap one.

Transubstantiation

Roman Catholic (the Orthodox also believe in a real change, just without the Aristotelian terms)

When the priest consecrates the bread & wine, their substance becomes the actual body & blood of Christ. But their accidents stay the same.

↳substance = what they fundamentally are
↳accidents = appearance, taste, texture

Sacramental union

Lutheran (often called "consubstantiation," though most Lutherans don't love that word)

Christ's body and blood are truly present in, with, and under the bread and wine. The bread stays bread—and Christ's body is really there with it, received by everyone who eats. Believer or unbeliever.

Spiritual real presence where I land

Reformed / Presbyterian (Calvin, the Westminster Confession)

Christ is present, but spiritually → we feed on Christ by the Holy Spirit. And when I say "spiritually," I mean by the Holy Spirit himself. As real as it gets.

↳Christ's body stays in Heaven. The Holy Spirit unites Christians to Jesus through the act of eating in faith.
↳The minister offers a prayer of thanksgiving & consecration, asking God to set apart the bread & wine through the Holy Spirit for holy use.

Memorial

Baptists, most evangelicals, non-denominational churches (going back to Zwingli)

"Communion is a symbolic act." We do it to remember what Jesus did and to obey his command. The bread and wine point to Christ, and that's where it ends.

↳ seems more non-denominational. It's the view I push back on up top.

Protestant vs. Catholic

Protestant (Reformed) arguments → how a Catholic would typically answer → the typical response

Where is Christ's body?

Christ's body is a true human body—and it's in heaven. He ascended (Acts 1:9–11), and Heaven must receive him until the time comes for God to restore everything Acts 3:21. He had to be made like them, fully human in every way Heb 2:17.

A real human body has a location. If it can be whole on ten thousand altars at the same time... is it still a body like ours? And if it's something else now, is his humanity still like ours? So this turns into a Christology problem on top of a sacrament problem.

A Catholic would agree his body is in heaven, in its natural way of existing (Council of Trent). But they'd say he's present sacramentally—the way a substance is present, without taking up space like an object in a place (Aquinas). He stays whole, he stays in heaven, and the bread is changed into him.

And they'd point out that the risen body has abilities ordinary bodies lack. It walked through locked doors (John 20:19).

Either it has the properties of a body—size, shape, location—or it doesn't. If it doesn't, in what sense is it still a body? When Jesus proved he'd really risen, he used exactly those properties: Touch me and see; a ghost does not have flesh and bones, as you see I have Luke 24:39.

Yes, he went through doors. But he was always in one place at a time. And after the ascension, where does Stephen see him? Standing in heaven (Acts 7:55–56).

"This is my body"

He said it while his actual body was sitting right there at the table—unbroken, holding the bread. And in the same breath: This cup is the new covenant in my blood, which is poured out for you Luke 22:20. Nobody thinks the cup is literally a covenant.

Scripture names signs by what they signify all the time. Circumcision is "my covenant"... and "the sign of the covenant," back to back (Gen 17:10–11). The lamb "is the LORD's Passover" (Ex 12:11). "That rock was Christ" (1 Cor 10:4).

"The cup" just means what's in it—like saying you'll drink a glass. Matthew gives it straight: This is my blood of the covenant Matt 26:28.

The rock and the lamb come labelled as types (Paul calls those events examples in 1 Cor 10:6). The Last Supper comes with no label like that. And Paul calls the cup a real participation in Christ's blood (1 Cor 10:16).

Fine—grant that the cup means its contents. The contents still aren't literally a covenant. So at least one "is" in the words of institution is sign language, and grammar alone can't force the other one to be literal.

And participation can happen with zero change of substance. Same paragraph: Do not those who eat the sacrifices participate in the altar? 1 Cor 10:18. Idol feasts make you a participant with demons (10:20). Does idol meat turn into demons? Of course not.

Is it still bread?

Scripture keeps calling it bread and wine after the words. Jesus calls the cup this fruit of the vine after blessing it Matt 26:29. And Paul, right in the warning passage: whenever you eat this bread 1 Cor 11:26. Bread. Three times in three verses.

Scripture names things by what they used to be, or by how they look. Aaron's staff is still called a staff after it became a snake (Ex 7:12). At Cana, John calls it the water that had been turned into wine John 2:9.

Both of those actually help my side. In each case Scripture tells us first that a change happened—and the change was visible. A snake. Real wine. Scripture never tells us the bread changed into anything, and the senses notice no change at all.

John 6

The promise for believing and the promise for eating are the exact same promise. The one who believes has eternal life (6:47). Whoever eats my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life (6:54). Same result... same act. Augustine: believe, and you have eaten.

He even defines it early: Whoever comes to me will never go hungry, and whoever believes in me will never be thirsty John 6:35. And read literally, it proves too much. Every communicant would be saved. And the thief on the cross—who never took communion—would be lost.

The sermon escalates. Jesus moves from "believe" to "eat my flesh," and from 6:54 on, John switches to trōgō, a graphic verb for chewing. When the crowd takes him literally, he lets it stand. He lets many of his disciples walk away (6:66).

And in 6:63 he says "the flesh" counts for nothing, where all along he'd been saying "my flesh."

Leaving a literal reading uncorrected is John's pattern with figures. "Destroy this temple" (2:19). "Born again" (3:3). "Living water" (4:10). People take it physically every single time, and Jesus keeps the figure. Then he points to the spiritual meaning—which is exactly what 6:63 does: The words I have spoken to you—they are full of the Spirit and life.

And trōgō? John uses it for Judas eating ordinary bread too (13:18). Even Catholic teaching says Christ's body is never literally chewed. So nobody takes that verb literally. And listen to Peter's answer at the end: You have the words of eternal life John 6:68. The words.

The sign & the senses

A sacrament is a visible sign joined to an invisible grace. Take away the bread and there's no sign left. Westminster says transubstantiation overthroweth the nature of a sacrament WCF 29.6.

When Jesus turned water into wine at Cana, it tasted like wine (John 2:9–10). When he proved his body was real, he said "touch me and see." A change that's invisible on purpose? That has no parallel anywhere in Scripture.

The sign is still there—the appearances of bread and wine are the sign. Aquinas says there's no deception, because the appearances really are present. It's the intellect that has to be guarded by faith.

And the Incarnation looked like an ordinary man too (Isa 53:2). It took faith to see who he was.

The Incarnation actually fits the Reformed view. People saw a real man—and he was a real man. Their senses were right about what senses can judge. Only his divinity was beyond them. Transubstantiation says the senses are wrong about bread... which is exactly what senses are for.

The Reformed view follows the Incarnation pattern: real bread to the senses, real Christ received by faith. And if our senses can be wrong about bread, what does that do to the apostles' witness to the resurrection? It rests on what they had heard… seen with our eyes… and our hands have touched 1 John 1:1.

Church history

The substance/accidents framework is Aristotle's, and transubstantiation was formally defined in 1215 (the Fourth Lateran Council). Before that, you find sign language all over the place. Tertullian explains "this is my body" as the figure of my body. Augustine uses John 6:53 as his textbook example of a figure (On Christian Doctrine 3.16). Pope Gelasius I wrote that the substance of the bread and wine does not cease.

The word came late—but so did "Trinity." The belief is early. Ignatius of Antioch (c. 110) calls the Eucharist the flesh of our Saviour. Justin Martyr (c. 155) says it isn't common bread. Cyril of Jerusalem tells new believers not to judge by taste.

The Fathers say Christ is truly given at the Supper. We agree! Calvin affirmed a real feeding on Christ. Ignatius was fighting Docetists who said Christ never had real flesh at all—and we confess his true flesh too.

"Change" language in the Fathers can mean a change of use and power. Cyril says consecrated oil is no longer plain oil either... and nobody thinks oil gets transubstantiated. And when Augustine says Christ "carried himself in his own hands," he adds quodam modo: in a certain manner.

Election and the Supper

Look how many times Jesus says the same sentence in John 6 (tap to see it):

John 6:39the given

“…that I shall lose none of all those he has given me, but raise them up at the last day.”

John 6:40the believers

“…everyone who looks to the Son and believes in him shall have eternal life, and I will raise them up at the last day.”

John 6:44the drawn

“No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws them, and I will raise them up at the last day.”

John 6:54the eaters

“Whoever eats my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life, and I will raise them up at the last day.”

He makes the same promise four times. The ones the Father gives. The ones who believe. The ones the Father draws. The ones who eat. Jesus is describing one group of people four different ways. The eaters of verse 54 are the given of verse 39.

So if "eating" there meant a mouth taking a host... then every mouth that ever took one would be raised to life on the last day. Judas included. But if the eaters are the given ones, then "eating" is the name for what the elect do when they come to Christ. And the chapter closes on exactly that: no one can come to me unless the Father has enabled them John 6:65.

My stance is that our union with Christ Jesus begins before the altar. God chose us in him before the creation of the world Eph 1:4, and the Holy Spirit is the bond that joins us to him. Paul says God has already seated us with him in the heavenly realms Eph 2:6. So at the table, the Spirit lifts us up to him. Sursum corda. Lift up your hearts.

The Supper is a seal—like circumcision was a seal of the righteousness that he had by faith Rom 4:11. It's God putting his seal on people who are already his... and actually feeding them on Christ, through the Spirit. It's God saying "you're mine" with bread you can hold in your hand.
